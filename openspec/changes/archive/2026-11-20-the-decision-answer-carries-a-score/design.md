# Design — the decision answer carries a recommendation score

### D1 — The table is the sheet's last block, not a column inside the recommendation

The header must keep the verdict first (the shipped requirement `The answer is presented once, verdict first`), and a
number printed beside the recommendation would be read before the residue and the risk that set it. So the score is a
block of its own at the end: the owner who reads two lines still gets the recommendation, and the owner who compares
options gets the distances after the evidence.

Rejected: a `points` column inside the criteria table — the criteria are the owner's own yardsticks, and putting a
judgement beside them makes the two look like the same kind of claim.

Pinned by `decisions#everyOptionCarriesAScore`, which compares the skeleton's line numbers rather than the presence of
the block: both shapes contain the words, and only the order tells them apart.

### D2 — The points are read off the columns, never computed from the two views

The shipped rule is that the short-term and strategic views stay two views and are never averaged. A score computed
from them would be that average wearing a name, so the rubric reads its anchors off what the sheet already states —
*does it solve it*, the risk level, and the strategic question "removes the cause, or re-counts it". A number that
disagrees with those columns is a defect of the sheet, and the number changes.

The ban's sentence was restated from "never one score" to "never one averaged score", which is what it always meant
and what the new table makes ambiguous if left alone.

### D3 — A delta is a rule, not an invitation

An unqualified 0–10 invites an owner to read a one-point gap as noise and a four-point gap as a close call — the
reverse of what it should mean here. So the deltas carry their reading: 1 is a tie that must name its breaking clause,
2–3 is "clearly worse" priced by that option's own clause, and 4 or more says the row was carried by the escalation
rather than offered. A sheet whose recommendation is not the top row is refused, and says which half moved.

### D4 — The absolute value is the author's judgement, and says so

9 and 10 both mean "recommended, land it"; 0 and 1 both mean "refused". The claim the sheet makes is about ordering
and distance, not about a calibrated measurement — which is why every row names what set it, and why the case pins the
six anchors: a rubric with a missing anchor is a feeling with a colour.

## Open decisions

none

The table's columns are `option | name | points | what set it`, and D1's order — the table last, after the
